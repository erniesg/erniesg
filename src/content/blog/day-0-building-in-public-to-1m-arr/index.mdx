I am writing this before anything is coherent enough to be impressive, which is probably the point.

The goal is simple to state and not simple to achieve:

**I want to build in public until I make $1M USD ARR.**

Or, more precisely: I want to build a portfolio of AI-native products, systems, and experiments until something, or some combination of things, becomes economically meaningful enough to give me real freedom.

$1M ARR is a slightly ridiculous number. It is not the same as $1M cash on hand. It is not the same as a stable salary. It is not the same as safety. But numbers are useful because they force vague desires to become explicit.

Right now, my mental frame is this: I would like to have either roughly **$1M on hand** or at least a **stable 5-figure monthly income** so that I can exercise the option of becoming a parent, if I choose that path, without diminishing my own quality of life.

I have become increasingly curious about parenting. Not as a cute lifestyle accessory, not as an imminent announcement, but as a serious long-term option I want to keep open. A child is not a side project. A family is not a Notion template. If I ever choose to take on that responsibility, I want to do it from a position of margin rather than panic.

Of course, in practice, I hope to build a life with someone else. This is not me cosplaying rugged individualism inside a family. But I keep thinking in terms of a single-income household stress test because I want the robustness buffer. I want the plan to still work if one income disappears, if care work expands, if someone needs time, if life does what life does.

Of course, wants are endless. Hedonic adaptation is real. Whatever number feels safe today may feel laughably insufficient tomorrow. But for now, this is the frame. Not because it is objectively correct, but because it gives me a direction to build towards.

## Why Now?

On a macro level, I no longer trust that the world's systems are built to look after my best interests.

This is not a dramatic anti-institutional manifesto. I still believe in public goods. I still believe in good people working inside flawed systems. I still believe many things are better solved collectively than individually.

But after my third trip to Europe, something in me shifted.

I felt strangely disillusioned. Not because Europe is one thing, or because Asia is magically better, or because I have discovered some grand civilizational truth after walking around with a suitcase and Google Maps.

It was more like this: the world suddenly felt like a series of badly marked crossings. You are told there is a safe path. You are told the bridge exists. You are told someone has checked the engineering. Then you arrive and realise everyone is improvising. The signs are outdated, the map is political, the people in charge are tired, and the cost of being wrong is borne very unevenly.

I do not think institutions are evil. I just think they are often slow, self-protective, and misaligned with the exact shape of one person's life.

And if I am going to make choices about work, family, geography, health, money, and the future, I would rather not be downstream of systems that may or may not care whether I land well.

So this is not really about becoming rich.

It is about buying optionality.

Optionality to parent without panic. Optionality to say no. Optionality to work on things because they matter, not because rent is due. Optionality to move between Singapore, APAC, and wherever else the work takes me. Optionality to care for people without turning every act of care into a financial stress test.

Money is not the only form of agency, but it is a very portable one.

## My Starting Point

I do not have rich parents. I am a first-generation college graduate. I do not come from the kind of background where entrepreneurship is a family office asset allocation strategy.

That said, I also do not want to perform poverty cosplay.

I have benefited from many compounding micro-advantages.

In AI terms, I was **pre-trained with unconditional love**. I grew up with a predisposition towards optimism, and a natural inclination towards STEM, which the Singapore system happens to reward. I was lucky to be in environments where effort often converted into opportunity.

Then came the **RLHF**: schooling, civil society, mentors, communities, projects, grants, newsroom chaos, museum experiments, hackathons, internet friends, and the many people who made me slightly less wrong over time.

And now there is **test-time compute**: the ability to sit with ambiguity, chase down problems, use tools aggressively, work across domains, and convert anxiety into artifacts.

So no, I am not "self-made." Nobody is.

But maybe that is what makes this journey more useful to document. I am not starting from zero, but I am also not starting from a trust fund. I am starting from a weirdly specific bundle of advantages, gaps, scars, skills, taste, and delusions.

Which is probably where a lot of people start.

## How I Plan to Build

The first principle is:

**Build the thing that builds the thing.**

Before I obsess over any single product, I want to build my own execution system.

For me, that starts with **Rucksack**: an issue-to-PR autopilot / human-gated agent system for turning messy intentions into shipped work. The point is not to replace judgment. The point is to reduce activation energy.

I want a system where ideas become issues, issues become branches, branches become PRs, PRs collect evidence, and I stay in the loop where human judgment actually matters.

Because the bottleneck is no longer just "can I code this?"

The bottleneck is: can I decide what matters? Can I keep moving when context-switching? Can I run multiple experiments without losing the plot? Can I design a machine that makes me more prolific without making me more chaotic?

Rucksack is the infrastructure layer.

![Rucksack execution loop: messy intent becomes issues, agent plans, branches, evidence, pull requests, human review, and shipped work, with learning feeding back into the backlog.](./rucksack-diagram.svg)

The loop matters more than any single automation. If Rucksack works, it turns vague intent into reviewable artifacts, and reviewable artifacts into shipped work across every other bet.

Then come the sidequests.

### Accrue

Accrue is the boring-but-potentially-useful one: AI workflows for accounting, bookkeeping, invoices, categorisation, Xero-style integrations, and human review gates before anything touches serious financial or regulatory actions.

This is not the sexiest surface area, which is exactly why it might matter. Boring workflows have budgets. Accountants have pain. Repetitive errors have economic cost. If AI can make the messy middle more reliable, there may be a real business here.

### Aether

Aether sits closer to creative operations: design, content, social listening, key visual propagation, multilingual adaptation, and the problem of helping teams produce more coherent creative output without needing 17 disconnected tools.

This is where my media, newsroom, AI, and design instincts collide. I do not know yet whether it is a product, a studio system, an internal tool, or a set of workflows that become a product later. But I know the pain is real.

### Tong

Tong is my language / culture / learning world.

I keep coming back to multilingualism, characters, stories, Chinese, English, translation, memory, and how people actually learn across symbolic systems. Tong may become a CJK learning product, a narrative game, an AI tutor, or something stranger.

It is probably less obviously monetisable than Accrue, but it is closer to my soul. So it stays in the portfolio.

### Remora

Remora is in the orbit of attachment, leverage, and small systems that ride on larger systems.

I like the metaphor because so much of useful software is not about replacing the whale. It is about finding the awkward, high-friction surfaces around existing workflows and attaching just enough intelligence there to make the whole thing move better.

I do not yet know whether Remora becomes a product, a class of integrations, or a recurring pattern across the portfolio. But it belongs here because the instinct feels important: build where distribution, dependency, and workflow gravity already exist.

### Stet

Stet is the editing / revision / publishing side of the house.

The word itself means "let it stand", which feels right for a world where AI can generate infinite drafts but the scarce thing is still judgment. What should be changed? What should be restored? What should be left alone?

This is the part of the portfolio that keeps pulling me back to writing, editorial systems, versioning, taste, and the last-mile human decisions that turn output into something publishable.

### New AI-Native Experiments

And then there will be new things.

The rule is not "only build what I already understand." The rule is to keep exploring AI-native products with plausible monetisation potential.

AI-native does not mean "add chatbot." It means the product could not really exist in its current form without models, agents, retrieval, generation, multimodal interfaces, or adaptive workflows.

Some experiments will be too early. Some will be dumb. Some will be good but not businesses. Some will be businesses but not for me.

That is fine.

The point is to increase my surface area for luck while making sure I am learning from reality, not just from my own imagination.

## What I Will Share

I do not want this to become performative founder content.

So I am not promising daily motivational posts about "shipping hard" while quietly avoiding the numbers.

I want to share:

- what I am building
- what I shipped
- what broke
- what users said
- what I thought would work but did not
- what I am learning about distribution
- what I am learning about pricing
- what I am learning about AI-native product design
- what I am learning about myself as the system running the system

I will probably share revenue when there is revenue worth sharing. I will share dead ends too, because dead ends are data.

The goal is not to look inevitable.

The goal is to make the path inspectable.

## Why Build in Public?

Because I want accountability, but not the toxic kind.

I do not need strangers yelling "ship it" at me every day. I need a public trail of decisions, experiments, and evidence. I need to be able to look back and see what changed.

Also, I suspect the path from "AI engineer with too many ideas" to "portfolio of profitable AI-native products" is going to become increasingly common.

Not everyone wants to raise venture capital. Not everyone wants to manage a 50-person team. Not everyone wants to choose between employment and entrepreneurship as if they are mutually exclusive religions.

Some of us want to build small, powerful, profitable systems.

Some of us want to stay close to the work.

Some of us want to use AI not just as a product layer, but as leverage for how we live.

## Day 0

So this is Day 0.

No grand launch. No polished landing page empire. No "I made $37,421 while sleeping" screenshot.

Just a line in the sand.

I want to build in public until I make $1M USD ARR.

I want to find out whether a first-gen, Singapore-shaped, AI-pilled, systems-obsessed, occasionally delusional builder can turn a cluster of tools, taste, and stubbornness into real economic freedom.

I want to see if the machine can build the machine.

And I want to document the whole thing clearly enough that someone else can reuse the parts that work.

If you are building AI-native products, dealing with accounting or creative workflow pain, learning Chinese, thinking about parenting and money, or just curious to watch someone route existential anxiety into shipping velocity, follow along.

This is the beginning.
